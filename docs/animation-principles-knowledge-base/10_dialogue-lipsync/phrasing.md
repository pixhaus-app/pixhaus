# Phrasing — Dialogue as Music

> Think of words, the shapes of words and phrases
The single most important insight about dialogue animation: people don't speak letter-by-letter. They speak in **phrases**. Animators who animate every letter look fake. Animators who animate phrases look alive.

## The "elephant juice" trick

the demonstration: try silently mouthing "elephant juice" to someone. They will read it as "I love you." The mouth shapes look identical.

This proves: **mouth shapes don't define words.** Context defines words. The audience reads phrasing, not phonemes.

## What this means for animation

Stop animating every consonant. Stop animating every vowel. Instead:

1. **Identify the major accents** in the line of dialogue
2. **Find the IMPORTANT vowels** that need clear mouth shapes
3. **Hit those clearly**
4. **Smear or skip everything else**

The voice actor will catch the "blossoms" in the middle of the line — those are the only mouth shapes that need accurate animation.

## of two

> Voice actors catch the impacts and shines in the middle of the whole pass. They match the first vowel at the start of the phrase and the last accent. And what's in the middle will work.
For a 2-second line of dialogue:
- Animate the **first vowel** of the phrase clearly
- Animate the **last accent** clearly
- Everything between is more flexible

This is the opposite of the schoolteacher approach (animating every "P" with a clean closure). It's how working professionals actually animate.

## The classic mistake — animating every letter

The infamous "PUH PUH PUH" approach. A 6th-grade teacher's diction class. Looks like:

```
"M-OO-V-A-B-LE L-IP-S"
```

Each letter gets its own mouth shape. Each one is held for 2 frames. The result: a robotic, stilted, "puppet" performance.

## The phrasing approach — example

For the word "fortune":

**Wrong (mechanical):**
```
F → O → R → T → U → N → E
[7 distinct mouth shapes, each held 2 frames]
```

**Right (phrasing):**
```
F → (smear through "ortu") → N → E
[2 or 3 distinct shapes total]
```

The "F" is the start. The "N" is mid-word emphasis. Everything else flows.

For "hello":
```
[Open H/E shape] → [Closed L shape] → [O shape]
3 distinct shapes, not 5
```

For "memory":
```
[M closure] → [E open] → (smear to O) → (smear to RY)
The line opens once, on the E. No re-opening for O.
```

## Important consonants to hit

These ALWAYS need 2+ frames to read:

- **M** (lips together — the lip closure is critical)
- **P** (lips together with puff release)
- **B** (lips together with voiced release)
- **F** (lower lip on upper teeth)
- **V** (lower lip on upper teeth, voiced)
- **T** (tongue tip on roof of mouth — but the tongue moves so fast you only need to suggest)

These closures are the "punctuation" of the mouth animation. Without them, words slur together.

## Vowels — explode INTO them

> To get fresh dialogue, you must jump (explode) INTO your vowels (not between them). Don't soften them. Soften them AFTER the accent. Hit it hard, then ease.
The shape:
```
ACCENT (hit hard) → SETTLE (ease into next position)
[1-2 frames] [4-8 frames]
```

Don't ease into a vowel — that softens the read. Hit the vowel sharply at peak open, then let the mouth relax through subsequent frames.

## Skipping intermediate positions

For an open vowel like "WOW" or "HEY," don't draw multiple intermediate shapes between closed and open. **Lose the intermediates.**

```
Wrong: Right:
[closed] → [40%] → [70%] → [open] [closed] → [open]
4 drawings, sluggish 2 drawings, snappy
```

The slow opening reads as "sappy and soft." Direct cuts read as "alive."

## The volume of the mouth

A common error: not stretching the lower face enough during dialogue. *"This makes it stiff and affected."*

The mouth needs to:
- **Open WIDE** for emphasized vowels (more than feels natural to draw)
- **Compress** for closures (more than feels natural)
- **Distort** during transitions (squash and stretch applies to mouths)

A timid mouth animation looks dead. The mouth must move with conviction.

## Phrasing — by a slow-articulating actor

Price had a slow, dramatic delivery. **Because his delivery was slow, every phoneme had time to be articulated.**

This taught dialogue PACE determines how much you can animate.
- **Fast delivery** → skip most positions, hit only major accents
- **Slow delivery** → can articulate each major phoneme
- **Sung delivery** → animate every vowel precisely (singing reveals open vowels)

## The phrasing checklist

For any line of dialogue:

1. ☐ **Where does the breath start?** (Anticipation pose with mouth slightly open, ready)
2. ☐ **What's the FIRST important vowel?** (Hit it clearly)
3. ☐ **What's the LAST accent of the phrase?** (Hit it clearly)
4. ☐ **What are the major closures (M, P, B, F, V)?** (Each needs 2+ frames)
5. ☐ **Where are the optional/skipped letters?** (Smear or omit)

## Prompt-ready language

### Video model — phrased dialogue
> "Character says 'Well, at last you're home!' The dialogue is phrased — three accents: WELL, LAST, HOME. The 'at' and 'you're' are smeared between accents. Mouth animation hits the WELL clearly with open mouth, smears through 'at last', hits LAST with strong vowel, smears through 'you're', closes briefly on the H, opens emphatically on HOME. Don't animate every letter — animate the rhythm."

### Video model — single-word emphasis
> "Character says the word 'memory' with a single open vowel. Mouth opens once on the 'E' (mem-EH-mory), then closes through 'mory' without re-opening. Only one wide-mouth shape in the whole word."

### For phoneme-level control (if needed):
For singing or precise lip-sync work, generate keyframes per important phoneme. Otherwise, just describe the rhythm.

## The the classical tradition summary

> The key to lip sync is the feeling of the WORD, not individual letters. The idea is not to be too active. Get the shape of the word and check that it reads. Select what is important and avoid fluttering the mouth animating every detail.
