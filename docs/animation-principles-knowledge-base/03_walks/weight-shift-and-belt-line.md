# Weight Shift and the Belt Line — How the Body Carries the Walk

The legs alone don't sell a walk. The mass above them does. ## The belt line / waist line technique

Imagine a horizontal line through the character's waist (the classical tradition calls this the "belt line"). Track this line through the walk. In any good walk, **the belt line tilts** — it's never level.

```
DOWN pose: PASSING pose: UP pose:
 ___
\_/ ___ / \
 / \ 
weight on bent leg level briefly weight on push-off
belt line tilts belt line level belt line tilts
toward bent leg toward push leg
```

## The waist tilt cheat sheet

| Pose | Belt line behavior |
|------|---------------------|
| CONTACT | Tilts toward the back (off-weight) leg |
| DOWN | Tilts strongly toward the bent (weight-bearing) leg |
| PASSING | Brief level moment as weight transfers |
| UP | Tilts toward the pushing-off leg |

A character with a level belt line throughout the walk looks like they're sliding, not walking.

## The shoulder counter-tilt

While the hips tilt one way, the shoulders tilt the **opposite** way. This is the natural counter-rotation of the spine.

- Hips tilt right? Shoulders tilt left.
- Hips tilt left? Shoulders tilt right.

This creates the natural S-curve in the spine that makes a walk feel alive. Without it, the body looks like a stiff plank.

## The chest-pelvis opposition

In any normal walk, the chest and pelvis rotate in **opposite directions** around the spine:

- Right leg forward → pelvis rotates so right hip leads
- Simultaneously, chest rotates so **left shoulder leads** (the right arm swings forward)

This is why an arm swings opposite to the leg on the same side. The shoulder rotation drives the arm.

## How to read a walk by its torso

follow someone down the street, watching only from behind. Don't look at the legs — watch the **head, shoulders, and hips** only.

You can identify:
- Age (older = less torso rotation)
- Mood (depressed = collapsed chest, no hip swing)
- Personality (confident = open chest, swung shoulders)
- Gender presentation (the famous the classical tradition "tightrope walk" example — feminine walk has minimal up/down, hips slide side-to-side instead)
- Health (sick = sagging shoulders, drag)

> He was walking like he was on a tightrope, sliding along it.
That's how the classical tradition recognized a man walking 50 yards away with a feminine gait — by tracking only the head, which had zero up/down bounce. The legs were close together, so the body slid horizontally instead of bouncing.

## The "amount of bounce" is everything

This is the single most important variable in walk character design.

### Lots of bounce (large up/down on head)
- Macho swagger
- Excited / happy character
- Child (high energy, big motions)
- Heavy character (mass really moves)
- Cartoon walk
- Marching

### Minimal bounce (head stays level)
- Feminine glide (legs close, slide instead of step)
- Sneak walk (deliberately quiet)
- Old / tired (no energy for bounce)
- Ninja / professional
- Limited animation style (TV cartoon shortcut)

### No bounce at all (perfectly level head)
- Tightrope walking gait
- Slide / shuffle
- Ghost or floating character
- Animated character on a flat plane (deliberate stylization)

## The the classical tradition formula (from observation)

These are levers to add character:

1. **Tilt the body** (lean in direction of travel)
2. **Use straight legs at contacts and when leaving positions** (go from straight to bent, or bent to straight — never bent to bent)
3. **Bend the body** — tilt shoulders and hips with shoulders opposite to hips when they rotate
4. **Drop the knee inward or outward** (knock-knee'd or bow-legged for character)
5. **Tilt the belt line favoring the lower leg**
6. **Drop the foot** (let the foot dangle in the lift portion)
7. **Delay the foot and toe on the ground until the last instant**
8. **Continuously tilt and tilt back the head** (head reacts to each step)
9. **Delay parts — don't make everything happen at once**
10. **Counterbalance fat, buttocks, breasts — delay clothes, pants, hair**
11. **Break the joints** (knees, elbows, wrists go past their straight position briefly)
12. **Lots of ups and downs (for weight)**
13. **Use different timing on legs, arms, head, body**
14. **Twist the foot — keep it out of parallel**
15. **If you have a normal action pose and alter a small part, you get something different**

## Prompt-ready phrases

### For "weighty" walk
> "Walk has clear up-and-down motion of head and torso. Belt line tilts with each step — favoring the bent weight-bearing leg. Shoulders counter-rotate opposite hips. Body leans slightly forward. Head bobs noticeably with each footfall."

### For "light / feminine glide" walk
> "Walk with minimal head bobbing — body slides horizontally rather than bouncing. Legs cross close to each other. Hips sway side-to-side. Belt line tilts but head remains nearly level. Smooth flowing motion."

### For "sneaking" walk
> "Each step is taken with caution. Body weight stays back as the foot reaches forward, then weight transfers slowly. Head stays mostly level — no bounce. Knees are bent throughout. Long held passing pose between each step."

### For "swaggering / macho" walk
> "Wide stance, legs apart, large up-and-down of the body. Shoulders rolling with each step. Hip swagger. Head moves significantly up and down. Body language is open and confident."
